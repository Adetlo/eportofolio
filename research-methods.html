This unit introduced the fundamentals of research methods, focusing on the scientific method and ethics in computing research. The three main purposes of research were explored: to explore (familiarise with a topic), to describe (report findings), and to explain (answer "why").

Scientific Method & Reasoning Types

Deductive Reasoning:

  • Moves from idea → observation → conclusion
  • Example: "All men are mortal" → "Jason is a man" → "Jason is mortal"
  • Can achieve perfect conclusions if all assumptions are 100% correct
  • More difficult in non-lab settings due to need for fully agreed-upon facts

Inductive Reasoning:

  • Moves from observation → analysis → theory
  • Example: "I break out when I eat peanuts" → "This is an allergy symptom" → "I am allergic to peanuts"
  • More frequently used for everyday problem-solving
  • Conclusions may not always be right, but more practical for partial information

Research Ethics - Menlo Report Principles

  • Respect: Participants must take part voluntarily with consent and right to anonymity
  • Beneficence: Balance between maximizing research benefit and minimizing participant risk
  • Justice: Equal treatment of participants and fair distribution of research benefits
  • Respect for Law and Public Interest: Legal due diligence, accountability, integrity standards

Required Reading

  • BCS Code of Conduct
  • Data Protection and Intellectual Property guidelines
  • Andersen & Hepburn (2020) - History of Scientific Method
  • Finn & Shilton (2023) - The Menlo Report

Collaborative Discussion 1: Ethics in Computing

Initial Post by Ade Putra Tio Aldino - Tuesday, 28 October 2025, 4:30 PM

Case Study: The Programmer's Dilemma - Software Quality vs. Deadline Pressure

I have selected the ACM case study involving a software programmer who discovers critical security vulnerabilities in a system shortly before its scheduled release. Management pressures the programmer to launch on time, arguing that patches can be deployed post-release (ACM, no date).

Application of ACM Code of Ethics

This scenario directly engages several principles from the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct. Most critically, Principle 1.2 (Avoid Harm) requires computing professionals to minimize negative consequences, particularly regarding system security (ACM, 2018). The programmer faces a conflict between organizational pressure and professional responsibility to protect public safety. Additionally, Principle 2.5 (Comprehensive and Thorough Evaluation) mandates that professionals must evaluate systems comprehensively before deployment, which rushing to release would violate.

Comparison with BCS Code of Conduct

The BCS Code of Conduct addresses similar concerns through its Public Interest clause, which states that members shall "have due regard for public health, privacy, security and wellbeing of others and the environment" (BCS, 2021). Both codes prioritize public welfare over commercial interests. However, the BCS explicitly emphasizes the duty to relevant authority, requiring members to raise concerns through appropriate channels within their organization, providing clearer guidance on escalation procedures than the ACM code.

Legal and Social Implications

Legal Considerations: Releasing software with known vulnerabilities could constitute negligence under product liability law and potentially violate data protection regulations such as the UK Data Protection Act 2018 and GDPR, which mandate appropriate security measures for personal data (Legislation.gov.uk, no date). Organizations may face significant fines and legal action if breaches occur due to known, unpatched vulnerabilities.

Social Impact: Deploying insecure software endangers users' data, privacy, and potentially their safety in critical systems. This erodes public trust in technology and the computing profession, creating broader societal harm (Fjeld et al., 2020). The decision demonstrates how individual professional choices have cascading social consequences.

Professional Implications

The programmer's professionalism is tested through this dilemma. Succumbing to deadline pressure compromises professional integrity and violates the duty of care expected of computing professionals (Correa et al., 2023). True professionalism requires courage to advocate for ethical positions even when facing organizational resistance. As Finn and Shilton (2023) note in their analysis of the Menlo Report, researchers and practitioners must balance beneficence with respect for stakeholders—in this case, end-users who trust the system's security.

Ethical Path Forward:

  • Document the vulnerabilities thoroughly
  • Formally escalate concerns to senior management with clear risk assessments
  • Refuse to certify the system as ready for deployment
  • If organizational pressure persists, seek guidance from professional bodies
  • In extreme cases, consider whistleblowing through appropriate legal channels

Research Questions, the Literature Review and the Research Proposal

Week 2 • October 2025

This unit introduced the various aspects of a research proposal, starting with defining a research question. Defining a research topic may appear "daunting" (Boza, 2022), but it is essential for successful research. The process starts with an initial research idea, then asking pertinent questions that may need further revisions before preparing the research proposal.

Research Question Formulation

Key Considerations for a Suitable Research Topic:

  • Specificity: The question should be focused enough to be answerable within the scope of the research
  • Relevance: The topic should contribute to existing knowledge in the field
  • Feasibility: The research should be achievable with available resources and time
  • Originality: The question should address gaps in existing research
  • Clarity: The question should be clearly articulated and unambiguous

Methods for Formulating Research Ideas:

  • Rational Methods: Systematic review of literature, identifying gaps, building on existing theories
  • Creative Methods: Brainstorming, mind mapping, exploring interdisciplinary connections

Literature Review Process

A literature review is a survey of scholarly sources on a specific topic. It provides an overview of current knowledge, allowing identification of relevant theories, methods, and gaps in existing research.

Steps in Conducting a Literature Review:

  1. Define the scope: Establish clear boundaries for your review
  2. Search for literature: Use databases, keywords, and citation tracking
  3. Evaluate sources: Assess quality, relevance, and credibility
  4. Analyse and synthesise: Identify themes, patterns, and gaps
  5. Structure the review: Organize findings logically
  6. Write and revise: Present findings clearly with critical analysis

Research Proposal Structure

The research proposal serves as the foundation for research. Key components include:

  • Title: Clear, concise description of the research
  • Introduction: Background and context of the research problem
  • Literature Review: Survey of existing research and identification of gaps
  • Research Questions/Objectives: Clear statement of what the research aims to achieve
  • Methodology: How the research will be conducted
  • Timeline: Schedule for completing the research
  • Expected Outcomes: Anticipated contributions to knowledge
  • References: Sources cited in the proposal

Required Reading

  • Ermel et al. (2021) - Literature review in scientific disciplines

Methodology and Research Methods

Week 3 • November 2025

Summary Post Research Proposal Review

Learning Outcomes

  • Understand the different research methods
  • Know which data collection methods are related to each method of research
  • Have some idea which of these would be suited to your area of research
  • Understand exploratory and descriptive research designs

This unit introduced methodology in general and provided an overview of research methods. Understanding that methodology choices are based on underlying assumptions is crucial. As Saunders et al. (2023) states, researchers have assumptions about realities (ontological), human knowledge (epistemological), and how values influence the research process (axiological). These assumptions shape how research questions are understood, methods used, and findings interpreted.

Research Design Types

Research design refers to having a plan about what you will do to answer your research question (Saunders et al., 2023). Two main types:

Exploratory Research:

  • Problem is not clearly defined
  • Cannot have conclusive results
  • Provides better understanding of the problem or situation
  • Example: Investigating effectiveness of CRM in mobile marketing

Conclusive Research:

  • More specific focus
  • Verifies insights and aids in selecting a course of action
  • Descriptive Research: Describes elements or causes in specified research area
  • Example: Critical analysis of social media as a marketing strategy

Research Methods Overview

Research methods are "systematic tools used to find, collect, analyse and interpret information" (Sage, 2021). Three main types:

Qualitative Research:

  • Gathers data about experiences, behaviours and emotions
  • Explores how or why something has occurred
  • Associated with inductive approaches
  • Tools: Focus groups, Case studies, Observations, Surveys, Interviews

Quantitative Research:

  • Gathers numerical data for statistical analysis
  • Discovers patterns and/or relationships
  • Questions: "How much?", "How many?", "To what extent?"
  • Associated with deductive approaches
  • Tools: Surveys, Design of experiments, IoT/sensor data, Big data

Mixed Methods Research:

  • Integrates both qualitative and quantitative research
  • Provides a holistic approach to research projects
  • Combines strengths of both methodologies

Data Collection Methods

Primary Research:

  • Information gathered directly from the subject
  • Original data collection through surveys, interviews, experiments

Secondary Research:

  • Data gathered from previously published primary research
  • Sources: Published case studies, articles, magazines, newspapers, books

Case Studies, Focus Groups and Observations

Week 4 • November 2025

Literature Review Outline Seminar: Privacy Case Study

  • Understand how to carry out case studies, focus groups and observation methods
  • Know which method would be suitable for your investigation
  • Consider the type of data you would obtain from each method
  • Discuss the advantages and drawbacks of these methods

This unit introduced data collection methods generally used for qualitative research but sometimes applied in quantitative research. Understanding when and how to use each method is crucial for effective research design.

Case Studies

Case studies involve in-depth research and study of individuals or groups. They lead to hypotheses and widen the scope of studying a phenomenon.

Limitations:

  • Should not be used to determine cause and effect
  • Cannot make accurate predictions (due to possible researcher bias)
  • Atypical respondents can lead to weak generalisations
  • May move away from external validity

Focus Groups

A commonly used qualitative research method where a group of people (6-10) express insights on the topic being studied.

Key Requirements:

  • Participants should have a common background
  • Comparable experiences among group members
  • Representative of target market
  • Aim: Find answers to "why", "what" and "how" questions

Observation Methods

Quantitative Observation:

  • Observing numerical values (weight, volume, shape)
  • From a sample representing target market
  • Objective data collection with statistical analysis
  • Methods: Experimentation, surveys (e.g., customer satisfaction)

Qualitative Observation:

  • Monitoring of characteristics
  • Researcher observes respondents from a distance
  • Respondents should be in comfortable environment
  • Provides in-depth insight into behaviours
  • Observer Roles: Complete observer, observer as participant, participant as observer, full participant

Interviews, Survey Methods, and Questionnaire Design

Week 5 • November 2025

Reflective Activity 2 Data Collection Reflection

  • Design a questionnaire to obtain the responses needed for investigation
  • Understand how the data obtained can be analysed
  • Know the difference between a good questionnaire and a poor one

This unit introduced the use of interviews, survey methods and questionnaire design. These methods are often used together to gain further insight into specific areas of investigation.

In-Depth Interviews

One of the most common qualitative research methods. It is a conversational piece involving only one respondent at a time, providing opportunities to get in-depth details from the respondent.

Key Characteristics:

  • One-on-one conversational format
  • Allows for deep exploration of topics
  • Flexible follow-up questions based on responses
  • Rich qualitative data collection

Surveys vs Questionnaires

Understanding the distinction between these two terms is important:

Survey:

  • Comprehensive method for gathering and analysis of data
  • One of the most important quantitative methods
  • Used to explore opinions, trends, etc.
  • Will involve a questionnaire as part of the process

Questionnaire:

  • Set of questions with answer choices used to conduct a survey
  • Valuable research instrument offering flexibility
  • Like a written interview
  • Can be carried out online, over the phone, or face-to-face

Survey Process (Cyclic): Survey Questionnaire → Survey Logic → Survey Distribution → Survey Data Collection & Analytics

Question Types

The type of questions used depends on the research approach:

  • Open Questions: Allow free-form responses (qualitative research)
  • Closed Questions: Predetermined answer choices (quantitative research)
  • Mixed: Combination of both (mixed methods research)

Note: Some questionnaires are better than others, and mistakes are easy to make, especially in technical areas.

Pre-Testing and Post-Testing

These methods can be used with any research method to present a viable "before-and-after" picture of implementing a new process or system.

  • Pre-Test: Baseline measurement before intervention/implementation
  • Post-Test: Measurement after intervention to assess change
  • Comparison: Enables evaluation of impact and effectiveness

Reflective Activity 2: Inappropriate Use of Surveys

Case Study: Cambridge Analytica and Survey Ethics

The Cambridge Analytica Scandal (2018)

Cambridge Analytica obtained and shared data from millions of Facebook users through innocuous surveys on Facebook. This represents the highest-profile case of surveys being used for alternative means, likely for monetary gains (Confessore, 2018).

How It Happened:

  • A personality quiz app ("This Is Your Digital Life") collected data from users who consented
  • However, the app also harvested data from users' Facebook friends without their knowledge or consent
  • Data was used for political advertising and voter profiling
  • Approximately 87 million users' data was compromised

Additional Examples of Inappropriate Survey Use:

1. OkCupid Emotional Manipulation Study (2014)

  • Dating platform manipulated match percentages shown to users
  • Users were not informed they were part of an experiment
  • Raised concerns about consent and potential emotional harm

2. Facebook Emotional Contagion Study (2014)

  • Facebook manipulated news feeds to study emotional responses
  • Nearly 700,000 users unknowingly participated
  • No informed consent was obtained

Impact Analysis from Multiple Standpoints:

Ethical:

  • Violation of informed consent principles
  • Breach of trust between platforms and users
  • Using data for purposes beyond original consent

Social:

  • Erosion of public trust in online surveys and research
  • Potential manipulation of democratic processes
  • Increased public awareness of data privacy issues

Legal:

  • GDPR implementation accelerated in Europe
  • Facebook fined $5 billion by FTC
  • Cambridge Analytica filed for bankruptcy
  • Strengthened data protection regulations globally

Professional:

  • Highlighted need for ethical guidelines in data collection
  • Increased scrutiny of research ethics in tech industry
  • Professional bodies updated codes of conduct
  • Greater emphasis on data governance and accountability

Key Lesson: These cases demonstrate that surveys and data collection must adhere to strict ethical standards, including informed consent, data minimisation, purpose limitation, and transparency. Researchers have a professional responsibility to ensure data is collected and used ethically, regardless of commercial pressures.

References

  • Confessore, N. (2018) 'Cambridge Analytica and Facebook: The Scandal and the Fallout So Far', The New York Times, 4 April.
  • Kramer, A.D.I., Guillory, J.E. and Hancock, J.T. (2014) 'Experimental evidence of massive-scale emotional contagion through social networks', PNAS, 111(24), pp. 8788-8790.

Quantitative Methods - Descriptive and Inferential Statistics

Week 6 • November 2025

Statistics Introduction Literature Review Due

Learning Outcomes

  • Understand and apply descriptive statistics
  • Identify the different levels of measurement

  • Define the different levels of quantitative data

This unit introduced quantitative methods, which examine relationships between variables measured numerically and analysed through statistical and graphical techniques. The unit of analysis is data, consisting of observed values of one or more variables of interest, organised into data sets.

Understanding Data Sets

A data set can be thought of as a table where:

  • Columns: Represent different variables
  • Rows: Represent individual observations
  • Cells: Contain the value that the variable takes for a given observation

To extract meaningful business intelligence from data, the methodology employed must be valid for the variable(s) of interest. The first important distinction concerns the level of measurement of each variable.

Levels of Measurement

Understanding the level of measurement is crucial for selecting appropriate statistical methods:

Nominal:

  • Categories with no inherent order
  • Examples: Gender, nationality, colour
  • Can only count frequencies

Ordinal:

  • Categories with meaningful order
  • Examples: Satisfaction ratings (poor, fair, good, excellent)
  • Differences between values not necessarily equal

Interval:

  • Ordered with equal intervals between values
  • No true zero point
  • Examples: Temperature (Celsius/Fahrenheit), dates

Ratio:

  • Equal intervals with a true zero point
  • Examples: Height, weight, income, age
  • Allows for meaningful ratios (e.g., "twice as much")

Descriptive Statistics

When exploring data, it is helpful to summarise data in meaningful ways. Descriptive statistics provides two main types of measures:

Measures of Location (Central Tendency):

Represents the value taken by "a typical observation" – an observation that falls "right in the middle of the data".

  • Mean: Sum of all values divided by the number of observations
  • Median: Middle value when data is ordered
  • Mode: Most frequently occurring value

Measures of Dispersion (Spread):

Provides an idea of the "variability" or "spread" of the data.

  • Range: Difference between maximum and minimum values
  • Variance: Average of squared deviations from the mean
  • Standard Deviation: Square root of variance
  • Interquartile Range (IQR): Range of the middle 50% of data

Graphical Summaries

One approach to summarising data is through graphical representation:

  • Histograms: Distribution of continuous data
  • Bar Charts: Comparison of categorical data
  • Box Plots: Show median, quartiles, and outliers
  • Scatter Plots: Relationship between two variables
  • Pie Charts: Proportions of a whole

Note: Unit 8 will deal more specifically with data visualisation.

Application to My Research

Reflection on quantitative methods for BNI London Branch study

For my research on Data Science in banking digital transformation, quantitative methods will be essential for:

  • Measuring Efficiency: Processing times, transaction volumes, error rates (ratio data)
  • User Satisfaction: Likert scale responses from staff surveys (ordinal data)
  • Performance Metrics: Before/after comparison of KPIs using measures of central tendency and dispersion
  • Visual Analysis: Graphs showing trends in operational efficiency over time

Understanding levels of measurement helps ensure appropriate statistical tests are applied to each variable type in my mixed-methods analysis.

Key Concepts

  • Quantitative methods examine relationships between numerically measured variables
  • Level of measurement determines appropriate statistical methods
  • Descriptive statistics summarise data through location and dispersion measures
  • Graphical summaries provide visual representation of data patterns

Inferential Statistics and Hypothesis Testing

Week 7 • December 2025

Literature Review Submitted Statistics Worksheets

Learning Outcomes

  • Apply inferential statistics to data analysis
  • Identify the correct probability distributions
  • Perform appropriate hypothesis tests
  • Understand the basic principle of probability

This unit explored inferential statistics and hypothesis testing. As Purdue University (2023) states, "The goal of inferential statistics is to discover some property or general pattern about a large group by studying a smaller group of people in the hopes that the results will generalize to the larger group." We make inferences about a population from a sample of data, and since data is inherently variable, all such inferences are subject to uncertainty quantified using probability.

Inferential Statistics Concepts

Statistical Inference:

  • Process of extracting meaningful information from raw data
  • Using sample data to make valid inferences about underlying population
  • Uncertainty quantified using probability

Hypothesis Testing:

  • Alternative form of inference using data to provide evidence about assumptions
  • Preferred when comparing relevant values between two or more populations
  • Tests whether some assumption about a population value is likely to be true

E-Portfolio Activity: Inference Worksheet Notes

Key concepts from inferential statistics

Four Steps of Hypothesis Testing:

  1. State the Hypotheses:
    • H₀ (Null Hypothesis): Statement about population parameter assumed to be true
    • H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): Contradicts H₀ (less than, greater than, or not equal to)
  2. Set the Criteria:
    • Level of significance typically 5% (behavioural) or 1% (medical)
    • Determines threshold for decision-making
  3. Compute the Test Statistic:
    • Mathematical formula determining likelihood of sample outcomes
    • Shows how far sample mean is from population mean
  4. Make a Decision:
    • p-value < 0.05 → Reject H₀
    • p-value > 0.05 → Accept H₀

Decision Errors:

  • Type I Error (α): Rejecting H₀ when it's actually true (false positive)
  • Type II Error (β): Accepting H₀ when it's actually false (false negative)
  • Power (1-β): Probability of correctly rejecting a false H₀

Key Principles:

  • Hypotheses must be set up before data inspection for impartial testing
  • Avoid claiming to have "proved" anything - only disprove if p-value = 0
  • Related samples tests are more powerful than unrelated samples equivalents

Validity and Generalisability in Research

Week 9 • January 2026

Validity Concepts PowerBI Exercise

Learning Outcomes

  • Understand how validity, generalisability and reliability affect investigation and research design
  • Consider how to analyse and present results to answer research questions
  • Apply business visualisation techniques using PowerBI

This unit focused on three critical dimensions within research: validity, generalisability, and reliability. These concepts affect any conclusions made after data collection and analysis, and must be considered before data collection begins. While reliability refers to the consistency of the measurement process, validity focuses on whether the measurement has the correct target—a far broader and more complex concept.

Key Research Concepts

Validity

  • Focuses on whether the measurement has the correct target
  • Internal Validity: Extent to which results can be attributed to the intervention rather than confounding factors
  • External Validity: Extent to which results can be generalised to other contexts
  • Construct Validity: Whether the measurement actually measures what it claims to measure
  • Content Validity: Whether the measure covers all aspects of the construct

Reliability

  • Refers to the consistency of the measurement process
  • Test-Retest Reliability: Consistency over time
  • Inter-Rater Reliability: Consistency across different observers
  • Internal Consistency: Consistency within items measuring the same construct
  • A measurement must be reliable before validity can be assessed

Generalisability

  • The extent to which findings can be applied to other populations, settings, or times
  • Affected by sampling method, sample size, and research context
  • Qualitative research often prioritises transferability over generalisability
  • Case studies may have limited generalisability but high contextual depth

Data Analysis Considerations

Qualitative vs Quantitative Data:

  • Quantitative: Leads to descriptive and inferential statistics; requires data cleansing and validation
  • Qualitative: Often down to interpretation; presents unique challenges in presentation
  • Data Cleansing: Process of detecting and correcting corrupt or inaccurate records
  • Data Validation: Ensuring data meets quality and integrity requirements before analysis

Project Management Fundamentals

Key Concepts:

  • Project Management: Controlling processes and activities related to a project
  • Computing Project: Any project dealing with computing infrastructure and information systems
  • Project Characteristics: Focused, time-specific, unique, varying environments
  • No "one size fits all": Methodology must match project context

Project Life Cycles:

  • Initiation: Define scope, objectives, stakeholders, feasibility
  • Planning: Develop detailed plans, schedules, resource allocation
  • Execution: Implement plans, manage teams, deliver outputs
  • Monitoring & Control: Track progress, manage changes, quality assurance
  • Closure: Handover, documentation, lessons learned

Project Methodologies:

  • Waterfall: Sequential phases, clear milestones, documentation-heavy
  • Agile: Iterative, flexible, customer collaboration, responding to change
  • PRINCE2: Process-based, controlled stages, defined roles
  • Scrum: Sprints, daily standups, product backlog, retrospectives
  • Hybrid: Combining elements from multiple methodologies

Risk Management

Understanding and managing project risks

Risk Fundamentals:

  • Risk-free projects do not exist
  • Risks cannot be completely avoided
  • Many risks not visible at project start
  • Significant risks must be calculated and planned
  • Risks can pose both threats AND opportunities

Risk Management Process:

  1. Risk Identification: Brainstorming, checklists, expert judgment, SWOT analysis
  2. Risk Analysis: Qualitative (probability/impact matrix) and quantitative assessment
  3. Risk Response Planning: Avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept
  4. Risk Monitoring: Track identified risks, identify new risks, evaluate effectiveness

Risks, Assumptions, and Constraints:

  • Risks: Uncertain events that may impact project positively or negatively
  • Assumptions: Factors considered true without proof (should be validated)
  • Constraints: Limitations or restrictions on the project (scope, time, budget, quality)

Change Management

Controlling changes within project life

Types of Change:

  • Corrective Action: Required to keep project on plan and within budget
  • Environmental Changes: Project itself needs to be amended
  • Requested Changes: Stakeholder or client requests

Change Control Process:

  1. Change request submission and logging
  2. Impact assessment (scope, time, cost, quality)
  3. Review and approval/rejection by Change Control Board
  4. Implementation of approved changes
  5. Documentation and communication
  6. Baseline updates as necessary
